The robot chef who cooks words

A model is a robot chef. You give it some words, and it cooks up the next word, then the next, one at a time, like stacking blocks. "Once upon a…" becomes "time". Every idea on this page is a trick to make that kitchen faster, smaller or cheaper. The live instruments below show the real numbers.

  • Model tiers · Haiku, Sonnet, Opus

    Small chef, big chef

    Haiku is a small chef: very fast and cheap, great at easy jobs like "is this banana yellow?" Sonnet is a middle chef for most jobs. Opus is a big chef: slower and pricier, but it can solve hard puzzles.

    You don't hire the fancy chef to make toast.

  • Dense vs Mixture-of-Experts

    Only the cake cooks come

    Dense: 100 cooks, and all 100 stir every pot. Mixture-of-Experts: each cook has a specialty. A helper at the door, the router, says "This is cake! Only the 2 cake cooks come." The other 98 rest.

    Same big kitchen, much less work per dish.

  • KV cache · GQA, MLA

    The chef's notes

    While reading your long story, the chef writes notes about every word so it never has to reread. A longer story means more notes, and lots of kids asking at once means a mountain of notes. GQA and MLA write the notes smaller so they fit on the table.

    Long stories need lots of table space.

  • Quantization

    A lighter recipe book

    The chef's recipe book is huge and heavy. Quantization writes "2 cups" instead of "2.000000 cups". The book gets light enough for a small backpack, meaning a small computer, and the food tastes almost the same.

    Fewer digits, same soup.

  • Speculative decoding

    The little helper guesses

    A little helper quickly guesses the next four words: "the cat sat on…". The big chef checks all four at once: "Yes, yes, yes… no, it's the mat." Checking is quicker than writing every word yourself.

    Same answer, served sooner.

  • Thinking budget

    Thinking quietly first

    Before a hard riddle, the chef can think quietly. More thinking gives better answers, but every thinking word costs a penny. After a while, extra thinking barely helps. A smart chef thinks a little and gets it right, while a smaller chef may think a lot and still miss.

    Think enough, not forever.

  • Cost per task

    The burnt-cake rule

    The cheap chef charges 5¢ a try but burns the cake half the time, and you have to clean up. The fancy chef charges 20¢ and the cake is almost always perfect. The fancy chef can end up cheaper.

    Pay for good cakes, not for tries.

  • Training stages

    How the chef learned

    1. Reads every cookbook in the world (pretraining).
    2. Copies great cooks (fine-tuning).
    3. People say "this dish is better than that one" (preference training).
    4. Practices puzzles and gets a sticker for each right answer (RL).
    5. Teaches a little chef its tricks (distillation).
Anatomy

One token's trip through the model

Every model in the news (Claude, GPT, Gemini, Llama, DeepSeek) is a decoder-only transformer at heart. A token goes in, passes through a stack of identical blocks, and a probability for every possible next token comes out. Then the whole thing repeats for the next token.

Almost every efficiency trick targets one of these stages. Tap a stage to see what costs money there and which technique attacks it.

Compute

Dense vs Mixture-of-Experts

In a dense model every parameter fires for every token. Double the size and you double the cost per token.

A Mixture-of-Experts model splits the feed-forward layer into many small "experts". A tiny router picks the top-k for each token, and the rest sit idle. You store a huge model but pay compute for a small one.

  • Good: more knowledge per FLOP, faster serving at scale.
  • Cost: every expert still has to sit in GPU memory, routing must stay balanced, and the training is trickier.
  • Frontier labs (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google) don't disclose their architectures. The public open-weight frontier has gone heavily MoE.

Memory

The KV cache: why long context is expensive

Attention needs the keys and values of every previous token. Recomputing them for each new token would be wasteful, so the server caches them. That cache grows linearly with context and with every user being served at once, and it often outgrows the model weights.

  • MHA (original): every query head has its own K/V. Most memory.
  • GQA: groups of query heads share one K/V head. This is the standard today.
  • MQA: one K/V head for everything. Cheapest, with some quality loss.
  • MLA (DeepSeek): compresses K/V into a small latent vector per layer.

Compute has the same problem. Full attention scores every pair of tokens, so cost grows with n². Sliding-window layers only look back w tokens.

Memory

Quantization: fewer bits per weight

Weights are trained in 16-bit, but serving often stores them in 8 or 4 bits. Decoding one token at a time is usually limited by memory bandwidth, not arithmetic: the GPU waits to read the weights. Halving the bytes roughly halves that wait.

Rule of thumb: 8-bit is close to lossless, 4-bit costs a little quality when done carefully, and below that quality drops quickly. Bigger models tolerate quantization better than small ones.

Latency

Speculative decoding

The big model's slow part is producing one token per pass. Checking several tokens in one pass costs about the same as producing one. So a small, cheap draft model guesses γ tokens ahead, and the big model verifies them all at once, keeping the agreed prefix and fixing the first miss.

The output is exactly what the big model would have produced. It just arrives faster. Expected tokens per big-model pass with acceptance rate α:

E = (1 − αγ+1) ÷ (1 − α)

Tokens / task

Thinking budgets: buying accuracy with tokens

Reasoning models (Claude with extended thinking or effort levels, OpenAI's o-series, Gemini Thinking) write a hidden scratchpad before answering. RL training on checkable tasks such as unit tests and math answers taught them to use that scratchpad well.

Accuracy rises with budget, then flattens. A bigger model often starts higher and plateaus higher, so at a hard task it can be cheaper per correct answer, even at five times the token price.

$ / task

Price per token is the wrong number

What you actually pay for is a task done right. A cheap model that fails more often costs you retries, and failures that reach a person cost their review time. Prompt caching cuts the price of a repeated prefix (system prompt, codebase, documents) to about 10% for cache reads.

cost / success = attempt ÷ p + failure_cost × (1 − p) ÷ p

Training

Where the differences between models are made

Architecture gets the headlines, but the recipe decides most of the gap between models. These stages run in this order, and each one buys something different.

  1. stage 1

    Pretraining

    Predict the next token over trillions of tokens of web, books, code and synthetic data. Data quality and mix matter as much as size.

    buys: knowledge, fluency
  2. stage 2

    Supervised fine-tune

    Imitate high-quality demonstrations of answering, tool use and following formats.

    buys: follows instructions
  3. stage 3

    Preference training

    RLHF, and at Anthropic Constitutional AI: learn which of two answers is better, judged by people or by written principles.

    buys: helpful, honest, safe
  4. stage 4

    RL on verifiable tasks

    Reward passing tests and correct math. This produces long-horizon reasoning and agentic coding.

    buys: reasoning, agents
  5. stage 5

    Distillation

    A large teacher's outputs train a smaller student, which is why small tiers keep getting better.

    buys: cheap, fast tiers
Choosing

Matching tier to task

Every lab ships small, medium and large tiers built from the same training lineage. The current Claude family is Haiku, Sonnet and Opus at version 5.5, plus Fable 5.1. Check Anthropic's model docs for each one's exact positioning and price. The usual pattern looks like this:

Classification, extraction, routing, high-volume chatSmall tier (Haiku-class)Latency and volume dominate. Distilled small models handle narrow tasks well.
Everyday coding, writing, analysis, agents with toolsMid tier (Sonnet-class)Best success-per-dollar for most work.
Hard multi-step reasoning, long autonomous runs, subtle refactorsLarge tier (Opus-class) with higher effortFewer failed attempts outweigh the higher token price.
